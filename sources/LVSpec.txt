Abstract:

Video Large Language Models (Video-LLMs) excel in video understanding but suffer from high inference latency due to autoregressive generation. Speculative Decoding (SD) mitigates this by applying a draft-and-verify paradigm, yet existing methods are constrained by rigid exact-match rules, severely limiting the acceleration potential. To bridge this gap, we propose LVSpec, the first training-free loosely SD framework tailored for Video-LLMs. Grounded in the insight that generation is governed by sparse visual-relevant anchors (mandating strictness) amidst abundant visual-irrelevant fillers (permitting loose verification), LVSpec employs a lightweight visual-relevant token identification scheme to accurately pinpoint the former. To further maximize acceptance, we augment this with a position-shift tolerant mechanism that effectively salvages positionally mismatched but semantically equivalent tokens. Experiments demonstrate that LVSpec is high-fidelity and rapid: it preserves

Summary Of Strengths:

S1: The paper accurately identifies and alleviates the "visual blindness" issue when existing loose SD methods designed for LLMs are applied to Video-LLMs. It clearly points out that undifferentiated verification based solely on linguistic priors ignores the varying importance of different tokens for visual information. Accordingly, it classifies draft tokens into three categories: visual-relevant, intermediate, and visual-irrelevant, and adopts a differentiated verification strategy, which well aligns with the core goal of Video-LLMs to ensure the fidelity of visual information.

S2: The paper is coherently and fluently written, with well-designed visualizations (e.g., Figures 1, 2, 3). [Question 1] How does text tokenization affect visual-relevance identification?

[Response] Thanks for the insightful question. The relevance computation in LVSpec is performed within the LLM backbone space and is not affected by the text tokenizer. In fact, we have conducted early experiments during the method design stage, and have empirically compared using last-layer hidden states versus text and visual embeddings as the relevance signal, which involves the influence of a text tokenizer, as summarized in Table 1.

Leveraging Hidden States (Choice of LVSpec)

Specifically, LVSpec computes relevance using hidden state representations in the LLM’s internal semantic space, making this process independent of the choice of subsequent tokenizer. Since video LLMs fuse textual and visual information within the LLM backbone, the last-layer hidden states of text and visual tokens can effectively reflect their semantic relevance.

Leveraging Tokenizer Embeddings

In contrast, we also experimented with computing similarity using text and visual embeddings (i.e., the outputs direct from the text tokenizer and the vision encoder, respectively). This strategy performs worse because these embeddings have not undergone semantic alignment within the LLM backbone and thus reside in different representation spaces.

By leveraging hidden states as relevance signal, LVSpec achieves better performance and is independant of a text tokenizer. We believe this intra-model and tokenizer-agnostic design is what enables LVSpec to generalize across various model settings.

[Question 2] Can visual-relevant words split into multiple subword tokens be misclassified as visually-irrelevant?

[Response] The subword tokens are indeed an interesting case to study! We investigate this issue and summarize several intuitive examples together with their corresponding visual relevance scores in Table 2. As a result, we do not expect visual-relevant words split into multiple subword tokens to cause visually relevant multi-subword words to be misclassified as visually irrelevant.

Specifically, we find that for words split into multiple subword tokens (e.g., 'clippings', 'barber', 'illuminating'), the first subword token still exhibits higher visual relevance, whereas the remaining subwords tend to receive lower relevance scores, sometimes even approaching those of punctuation (e.g., ':'). We consider this behavior reasonable: earlier subwords often constrain or determine the subsequent ones and therefore carry more semantic information.

We do not expect this to cause visually relevant multi-subword words to be misclassified as visually irrelevant, since (1) the first subword token remains highly discriminative, and (2) later subword tokens are generally unlikely to be mismatched in practice once the first subword is determined.

Paper Summary:

This paper proposes a new speculative decoding-inspired method to accelerate autoregressive decoding in video LMs, with negligible performance loss.

LVSpec applies speculative decoding (a very popular decoding-technique to accelerate inference in a lossless manner in LLMs) to video LMs. The basic gist of speculative decoding involves having a small draft model propose tokens, and a larger verifier model to verify the proposed sequence (the longer the number of accepted tokens, the more efficient the generation). However, vanilla speculative decoding requires strict exact match: the drafter must propose exactly the token that the verifier would generate next for it to be accepted. This strictness is not necessarily desirable for video LLMs, where (1) the tokens necessary for visual grounding are sparse, but most integral for good generation; and (2) the draft model may propose (or omit) innocuous tokens that induces a position shift.

To this end, the authors propose three lossy SD contributions: (1) they introduce a way to determine the most visual-relevant tokens by ranking the cosine similarity between draft / verifier hidden states. (2) This token identification scheme can be used for a visual-semantic guided loose generation that adopts stricter verification for more visually relevant tokens. (3) They introduce a position shift-tolerance mechanism that relaxes acceptance for tokens that appear in the draft sequence but at mismatched positions, and otherwise do not degrade visual information.

Experiment-wise, the authors chose two Video-LLM model pairs: Qwen 2.5-VL and LlaVa-OneVision, in 32B/72B (verifier) and 7B (draft) sizes, and test on four video captioning and video QA tasks. For baselines, they compared against naive SD, SpecLM (a baseline which applies video token pruning to the draft model), and FLy, another loosely SD method. They also report several efficiency metrics; e.g., token/s, speedup (relative to verifier), and mean accepted length tau. LVSpec outperforms all baselines by a substantial margin on efficiency, and shows a very small drop in performance retention across downstream tasks (although FLy, a baseline, is quite competitive in some cases).

Beyond, the authors also provide some nice analyses showing that not all tokens in V2T generation are relevant / critical for visual grounding, and conduct an extensive array of ablations and analyses in the appendices, which I really appreciate. In all, I think this is a good and carefully written paper, and am overall pretty positive about acceptance!

Paper Summary:

This paper tackles the slow inference speed of Video-LLMs. The authors argue that existing Speculative Decoding (SD) methods are too strict because they reject tokens that are different from the target but semantically correct. To solve this, they propose LVSPEC, a training-free "loosely" speculative decoding framework. The key idea is "Visual-Semantic Guidance." It calculates the similarity between text tokens and visual features.

High visual relevance: The token is strictly verified (must match exactly).

Low visual relevance: The token is loosely verified (accepted even if it mismatches, assuming it is just a filler). They also introduce a Position Shift-Tolerant (PST) mechanism to handle order differences. Experiments on Qwen2.5-VL and LLaVA-OneVision show about 2.7x speedup with over 99.8% performance retention on video benchmarks.

Summary Of Strengths:

Impressive Efficiency: The method achieves significant speedups (2.7x - 2.94x) on state-of-the-art models like Qwen2.5-VL-32B, which is a strong practical contribution.

Strong Motivation: The "Oracle Study" effectively demonstrates that only a small portion of generated tokens are visually important "anchors." This justifies the strategy of relaxing verification for "filler" words.

Training-Free: The approach does not require training extra modules or finetuning, making it easy to deploy.

Theoretical Support: The paper provides a mathematical analysis showing why loose verification can theoretically outperform the speed limits of strict verification.-

[Response] We have recognized this minor issue and have discussed it in both the Limitations and Appendix K. We acknowledge that some cases may involve tokens that are visually irrelevant yet logically critical (e.g., ``not''). We further address this concern from the following three perspectives.

(1) Our method primarily aims to explore the performance boundary of loosely speculative decoding on mainstream video understanding tasks, and to provide a empirically validated solution that is effective under common model settings. Although such cases can arise in theory, the proportion of visually irrelevant but harmful draft tokens is small in descriptive output patterns, and the impact on overall performance is limited according to empirical results according to Figure 6 and Table 7 in the paper.

(2) When the output patterns do contain substantial visually irrelevant yet logically critical tokens, improvements can be directly made upon LVSpec. First, we can augment the visual relevance metric with additional heuristics (e.g., entropy) to identify non-visual keywords. This would not undermine the role of the visual relevance metric since visually grounded tokens still remain critical. Second, when such tokens cannot be reliably identified, a simple fallback to strict verification can be used to avoid performance degradation.

(3) Potential failure cases are a shared issue in the area of loosely speculative decoding. JudgeDecoding [1] can degrade in quality when encountering out-of-domain inputs or output patterns unseen during training. FLy [2] treats a current mismatch as a synonym if it is followed by several matches, which may inadvertently accept drafts that preserve superficial structural consistency but are nonetheless lower-quality than the target model’s output.

[Response] Thanks for your response. It is important to reiterate the research scope of LVSpec. Our study focuses on inference for visual grounding tasks in Video-LLMs, and we argue that text-only tasks fall outside the research scope of our work. We believe this edge case is more relevant to practical deployment (mixed visual grounding and text-only queries) as an engineering setup, and we are happy to discuss it further. We will elaborate on this from the following perspectives:

Fundamental Differences Between Text-Only and Visual Grounding Study

Our research falls within the scope of visual grounding (visual understanding), which requires establishing correspondence between linguistic expressions and visual content and performing understanding or localization based on visual evidence (e.g., visual question answering, visual captioning). We believe the mentioned "text-only queries when an unrelated video is loaded" does not belong to this category. In essence, it remains a text domain task or edge case.

The most fundamental difference between text-only and visual grounding research lies in the interaction with visual content, which directly leads to substantial differences in their model training, task formulations, and technical approaches. Unlike LLMs, vision–language models (VLMs) require specialized designs to encode and process visual inputs and to account for the internal information flow between visual and textual modalities, rather than treating them as unrelated components. These distinctions clearly separate visual grounding research from text-only studies, making technologies for visual grounding both necessary and an important research area apart from the text domain.

Established Practice in Vision–Language Model Research

Moreover, existing research based on VLMs also typically focuses within the scope of visual grounding. Mainstream technical approaches such as token compression [1,2] and KV cache optimization for VLMs [3] are developed and evaluated on visual grounding tasks (e.g., visual question answering and visual captioning), and are rarely studied in text-only settings or when the video is unrelated to the query. Therefore, we believe that evaluating LVSpec on various visual grounding tasks is sufficient to demonstrate our contributions.

In conclusion, we believe that addressing the inference efficiency of VLMs with a simple yet effective approach is already highly promising. We understand that in practical deployment scenarios, VLMs may receive purely textual queries or queries unrelated to the video content. But that such cases fall outside the scope of our method rather than constituting its limitations, and are more appropriately handled by engineering effort such as system-level routing and fallback mechanism as you mentioned. To maintain the focus of our research, we concentrate on the properties and challenges of visual grounding. And we believe the community can build upon our strong empirical performance and simple yet effective method to pursue further research in this direction.
